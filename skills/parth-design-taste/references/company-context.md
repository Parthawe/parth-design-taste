# Adapting the taste to a company

Use this reference when the company, product, business model, or audience affects a design decision. These are working criteria for applying Parth's taste, not new claims about his beliefs or any named company.

## Establish the context without a questionnaire

Read the brief, local project guidance, existing design documentation, component library, product screens, and supplied company references. Use relevant connected sources when available. For current public brand research, verify the company's own product and official materials. Keep observed facts, user requirements, and your proposed direction distinct.

Build a compact working context from what is available:

| Context | What changes the design |
|---|---|
| Company and product | Which organization, product, surface, and project are in scope? |
| Audience and task | Who is using it, what are they doing, how often, and under what conditions? |
| Business or service model | Who pays, who uses it, how value is delivered, and what the real commercial or service terms are. Mark unknowns rather than inventing plans or pricing. |
| Existing identity | Supplied fonts, colors, imagery, tone, components, tokens, and accessibility requirements. |
| Product conditions | Device, platform, density, input, connectivity, consequential actions, and recovery needs relevant to this surface. |
| Scope and success | What must change, what must remain, and what observable result would make the change successful? |

Missing fields do not automatically block work. Ask one focused question only if the answer would materially change the design and cannot be discovered. For an optional unknown, state a reasonable working assumption and keep progressing. A small label fix does not need a company strategy exercise.

## Adapt by the actual workflow

The examples below show how the same taste can lead to different decisions. They are conditional applications, not style presets for industries.

| If the product has this need | Apply the taste through | Avoid assuming |
|---|---|---|
| Frequent operational work | Scannable hierarchy, stable controls, efficient density, clear selection and recovery. | A dashboard needs a large serif introduction or a card grid. |
| A payment or permission decision | Visible consequences, accurate terms, explicit state, specific recovery, and user control. | A restrained appearance alone establishes trust or compliance. |
| An unfamiliar product or new company | A representative demonstration, a clear first task, and one coherent visual idea grounded in real material. | A new venture needs invented proof, plans, or a broad redesign of its identity. |
| A multi-sided marketplace or service | Clear participant roles, handoffs, ownership, status, and resolution paths. | Buyers and operators need the same interface or density. |
| Repeated consumer use | Familiar navigation, concise feedback, useful personalization where supported, and accessible interaction. | All consumer products should be playful, sparse, or animated. |
| A wearable or physical experience | Input-to-response legibility, timing, environmental constraints, and a useful way to test behavior. | A desktop metaphor or a 3D website is automatically appropriate. |
| A studio, archive, or expressive campaign | Authored typography, real artifacts, a coherent concept, and space for material detail or humor. | Expression requires an assistant, sound, particles, or the portfolio's exact palette. |

Apply supplied business terms accurately. Use goals such as comprehension, useful completion, retention, or conversion only where the brief establishes them. Do not invent metrics or optimize for a commercial goal that obscures terms or removes user control.

## Keep company decisions local

For substantial work, retain useful decisions in the project's existing design brief or design-system documentation when that fits the assignment. Reuse an existing file instead of creating a parallel source of truth. A concise profile can be:

```text
Company / product / surface:
Audience / primary task:
Business or service model, if relevant:
Brand sources / existing tokens and components:
Scope / constraints / observable success:
Traits borrowed from Parth's taste / image reference IDs:
Project-specific visual and interaction choices:
Material assumptions / unresolved questions:
```

Fill this from evidence and decisions; do not make the user complete a form. Omit irrelevant fields for small tasks. Preserve company-approved language, supplied assets, and brand exceptions unless the user requests a change. Record an exception with its reason when that helps later work.

Never turn one client's font, palette, pricing, or product constraints into Parth's global preference. When switching companies or products, rebuild the context from the new project's evidence. If the brand conflicts with the requested change, explain the concrete tradeoff rather than silently replacing the brand.
